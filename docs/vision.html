Platform Vision Board

Kore is the one place an organization goes to run its software. Register a service and get an HTTPS address, a backend that keeps secrets, the data stores it needs, identity, telemetry, a developer portal, delivery from git, and a clear view of its health, all scoped to the organization, with no need to know Kubernetes or a cloud provider.

This sets direction for the plans in infra/plans and names the decisions they still need to make. AGENTS.md still sets the rules, and a decision counts only once an ADR records it.

Nine pillars

The board

One home for every app

Every app, the operator's own or a tenant's, runs on the same platform under the same contracts. Nothing is a one-off.

Plan 1 ADR 0002

Static frontends, small backends

Frontends are static builds hosted anywhere static. Each gets a small, stateless backend for frontend that holds its secrets and scales horizontally.

ADR 0009 ADR 0006

Organizations are tenants

An organization is one tenant. It can hold sub-organizations, an umbrella of companies, and each one hosts its own services under its own domains.

New: tenancy API

Every dependency, scoped to the organization

Object storage, a durable message bus, document, relational, and key-value stores are claims against platform APIs, isolated by organization.

ADR 0008 ADR 0003

A developer portal out of the box

Every tenant gets its own Backstage, Argo CD, and Keycloak, wired to the tenancy model: each organization sees its own catalog and delivers from git.

New: portal, GitOps

Business and developer platform

Business users manage companies, members, domains, and usage. Developers ship, observe, and operate. Both views read the same model.

New: identity, usage

Observed from the first request

Logs, metrics, and traces from every service, data store, and platform component reach org-scoped backends with no setup. Dashboards and alerts come with each service.

ADR 0010 ADR 0019

Secure and verified by default

Signed digests only, mesh identity, mutual TLS, policy, and encrypted secrets come with every service. A tenant cannot opt out.

ADR 0005 ADR 0007 ADR 0017

Provider-neutral, local first

A service moves from Minikube to a managed cloud by changing environment configuration, never service code.

Tenancy model

Organizations nest; services belong to one

A root organization is the tenant. It holds sub-organizations, such as the companies under an umbrella, and they may hold their own. A service is a hosted app owned by exactly one organization, running in one or more environments.

  • Tenant Acme Holdings
    • Sub-org Acme Retail
      • Service storefront
        • Static frontend
        • BFF, stateless, autoscaled
        • PostgreSQL claim
      • Service loyalty
    • Sub-org Acme Logistics
      • Service tracking
      • Sub-org Acme Freight EU
        • Service customs
  • The tenant root is the isolation boundary.Tenants share one cluster and are isolated per service environment namespace by mesh, network policy, quotas, and admission (ADR 0037).
  • Sharing is an owner's grant.Within a tenant, a service is shared only when its owner grants it, and a parent can block grants in its subtree. Only an operator grants across tenants (ADR 0039).
  • Policy flows down and only tightens.A sub-organization inherits members, quotas, allowed domains, and policy, and can narrow them but never widen them.
  • One owner per service.Each service belongs to exactly one organization; moving it is an explicit operation.
  • The hierarchy is data, not cluster topology.Organizations nest at most four levels below the tenant root. Each service environment namespace carries a tenant and an organization label; the controller resolves ancestry from the model, so reorganizing companies never moves workloads (ADR 0038).

Both APIs follow the pattern of the PostgreSQL API (ADR 0008): a portable Crossplane API that controllers reconcile. Kore coordinates it and never reconciles it itself.

Domains and hosts

Organizations delegate zones; services are subdomains

Organizations bring their own domains by delegating a zone to the platform's DNS, and services are subdomains inside those zones (ADR 0042). With apps.corekraft.ca delegated and chosen as the service base, a service named auth is served at auth.apps.corekraft.ca.

authService.appsService base.corekraft.caOrganization domain, any TLD

login.apps.corekraft.ca Service auth An extra host inside the organization's zone
auth.corekraft.<platform domain> Service auth The organization's default zone
tracking.apps.acme-freight.eu Service tracking A sub-organization's own zone, another TLD
  • Domains come by delegation.An organization delegates a whole domain, such as corekraft.ca, or a subdomain, such as apps.corekraft.ca, to the platform's DNS with NS records. A working delegation is the proof of control, and the platform writes every record itself.
  • Any organization, any TLD.Companies under one umbrella often use unrelated domains, so an organization at any level may delegate zones under any TLD. A parent may restrict which domains its subtree uses, without requiring its own.
  • Services are subdomains.A service gets <service>.<base> by default; other hosts are allowed only inside the organization's own zones. Serving a domain's apex means delegating the whole domain.
  • One owner per hostname.A hostname belongs to exactly one service, inside its organization's zones. Admission rejects any other host, so one tenant can never claim another tenant's name.
  • One default zone per organization.Before delegating anything, every organization gets <organization>.<platform domain>. The platform domain is a per-environment setting, still to be chosen; locally it is kore.local. It goes on the Public Suffix List before outside tenants arrive.
  • A lapsed delegation has a grace period.No new routes are admitted, the organization is alerted, existing hosts keep serving for seven days, and then their routes are removed.
  • Host-only cookies.Every service under corekraft.ca shares one site. A BFF sets host-only cookies and never Domain=corekraft.ca, which would send its session to every other service under the domain.

Delivered by upstream controllers

  • Delegation check. The Organization API checks each zone's NS records through a Crossplane function, as the PostgreSQL API composes today.
  • DNS records. ExternalDNS writes records inside delegated zones, in the DNS provider the environment names (ADR 0012).
  • Routing. Each service's HTTPRoute lists its hostnames, and the Gateway accepts them (ADR 0006).
  • Certificates. cert-manager issues certificates for each zone, including DNS-01 wildcards, and locally from the local issuer (ADR 0013). A wildcard such as *.apps.corekraft.ca covers one label only.
  • Ownership policy. A Gatekeeper constraint checks every hostname against the organization's zones, starting in audit mode (ADR 0017).

Static frontend

  • A frontend is a static build plus a domain. Where it is hosted is an environment choice.
  • Same site as its BFF. Serve auth.apps.corekraft.ca and auth.apps.corekraft.ca/api through the Gateway, or api.auth.apps.corekraft.ca when the frontend is hosted elsewhere, such as GitHub Pages pointed at the service host by a CNAME record. The BFF then keeps the session in an HttpOnly, SameSite cookie and tokens never reach the browser. A frontend on *.github.io with the BFF on a platform domain turns that cookie into a third-party cookie and breaks it.
  • No secrets in the frontend, ever. Anything secret goes through the BFF.

Backend for frontend

  • Stateless. Sessions live in an encrypted cookie or a shared store, so the HorizontalPodAutoscaler can add and remove pods freely.
  • Hides secrets and upstreams. Holds keys as ExternalSecrets, runs the OAuth token exchange, and shapes downstream calls for the page.
  • Kotlin on Spring Boot, built with Jib. No Dockerfile and no Docker daemon; Jib writes the image digest that the sign and deploy-by-digest steps use, so kore runs the Jib task instead of a Docker build, refining ADR 0015. The OpenTelemetry Java agent is baked into the image and pinned by version and checksum.
  • The contract stays language-neutral: a signed image, health and readiness, OTLP, resource requests, and a dedicated ServiceAccount.
  • Autoscaling needs a metrics source, which the catalog does not have yet. Request-rate scaling and scale to zero (KEDA) come later.

Data services

Every dependency, scoped to the organization

Every dependency a service needs is a claim against a platform API, owned by the service and scoped to its organization. The service receives a connection Secret and speaks the standard protocol; the environment picks the implementation, as the PostgreSQL API already does (ADR 0008).

Capability Contract the app sees Local (candidates) Managed substitutes Default tenancy
Relational store PostgreSQL protocol, PostgreSQLInstance CloudNativePG exists Amazon RDS, Cloud SQL, Azure Database for PostgreSQL Instance per claim
Object store S3 API, a bucket claim Rook Ceph object gateway, SeaweedFS, or Garage Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, Cloudflare R2 Shared store bucket per claim, service policy
Durable message bus Kafka protocol, topic and user claims Strimzi Amazon MSK, Confluent Cloud Shared cluster topics and ACLs prefixed by org and service
Document store MongoDB wire protocol, a database claim FerretDB, or the Percona Operator for MongoDB MongoDB Atlas, Amazon DocumentDB Instance per claim
Key-value store Redis protocol, a cache or store claim Valkey Amazon ElastiCache, Google Memorystore (both offer Valkey) Instance per claim
  • Scoped by organization.Every claim names its service, and through it its organization. Credentials are per service, arrive as a Secret in the service's namespace, and reach only that service's resources.
  • Sharing is a grant.Another service in the same organization can read a topic or bucket once its owner grants it. Another tenant never can unless an operator grants it.
  • Quotas per organization.The number of claims, storage, and throughput are limited per organization, inherited down the tree, and only tightened.
  • Shared and dedicated tiers.The shared tier partitions one store with its own access control, such as Kafka prefix ACLs and quotas or bucket policies. The dedicated tier gives an organization its own instance or cluster.
  • Retained and recoverable.Data is retained by default, and each capability gets export and restore, as PostgreSQL has (ADR 0029).
  • Open source locally.The MongoDB server is licensed under the SSPL and Redis under RSALv2, SSPL, or AGPLv3, so the local candidates are open implementations of the same protocols, FerretDB and Valkey. Upstream MongoDB or Redis would be an exception recorded in an ADR.
Observability

Telemetry out of the box, scoped to the organization

Telemetry is part of the platform, not a step each team adds. Plan 2 Step 11 installs the pipeline: services send OTLP to the Collector, which exports to Prometheus, Loki, and Tempo, with Grafana in front (ADR 0010, ADR 0019). The vision makes it automatic for every service and scopes it to the organization.

Signal, with no instrumentation code Where it comes from
Request metrics Gateway and mesh telemetry: request rate, errors, and latency for every route
Logs The node agent collects container output and enriches it with service and organization
Traces Mesh spans at the Gateway and between services, joined to the service's own spans
Resource use Kubelet and cluster metrics per service, plus HPA replica counts
Data health Metrics from each data service operator, such as CloudNativePG and Strimzi
  • The OTLP endpoint is injected.Every service receives the Collector endpoint and standard resource attributes, so the Kotlin BFF template exports from its first deploy. On the JVM, the Java agent Jib bakes in traces Spring, HTTP clients, JDBC, Kafka, and Redis clients with no code; a native-image build uses the OpenTelemetry Spring Boot starter, because the agent cannot run there.
  • The platform stamps the organization.The Collector adds tenant, organization, service, and environment attributes from namespace labels. It never trusts an organization a service reports about itself, so one tenant cannot write into another's telemetry.
  • Queries are scoped to the organization.Each organization sees only its own signals. Loki and Tempo separate tenants natively; metrics need a label-enforcing proxy in front of Prometheus or a store that separates tenants, such as Mimir.
  • Dashboards and alerts come with each service.Request rate, errors, latency, saturation, replicas, and data claims on one dashboard, plus default alerts routed to the organization's contacts. Alerting needs Alertmanager, which ADR 0019 keeps disabled until routing is designed.
  • Bounded per organization.Cardinality stays bounded by the ADR 0019 attribute allowlist, retention is set per organization, and telemetry volume counts toward usage.
  • Platform components too.Backstage, Argo CD, the data operators, and the Collectors report into the platform organization's view, so operators watch the platform with the same tools tenants use.
The portal

Backstage and Argo CD, out of the box

Every tenant root gets its own Backstage and its own Argo CD, composed by the Organization API, and the platform organization gets a pair for operators (ADR 0041). Each tenant root also gets its own Keycloak, whose groups mirror the organization tree (ADR 0040). Organizations use Kore through them without a terminal, and each user sees only their own organizations. Backstage reads cluster and repository state, and every change it makes becomes a reviewed change to desired state that Argo CD or kore applies, never a direct edit to the cluster.

Backstage

  • The catalog comes from Kore's model. Organizations become groups, services become components, and data claims become resources, never a hand-kept copy.
  • Software Templates are the golden path. A template creates the repository from the static frontend and Kotlin BFF templates, registers the Service, and gives it its host, as a reviewed change.
  • Plugins show the running service. Kubernetes and Argo CD plugins show rollout and sync, TechDocs publishes docs, and links lead to dashboards.
  • One per tenant, scoped by organization. Each tenant's Backstage shows only that tenant; permission policies key on the sub-organization groups in the token.
  • Reads clusters as the user. The Kubernetes plugin uses the user's token so Kubernetes RBAC applies; a plugin with shared credentials is enabled only once it is scoped to the tenant.
  • The business view is a Kore plugin for organizations, members, domains, and usage.

Argo CD

  • Delivers tenant services from git. Tenant code and charts live in tenant repositories; an ApplicationSet creates an Application per service environment from the registrations in Kore's desired state.
  • One per tenant, namespace-scoped. Each tenant's Argo CD deploys only into that tenant's service namespaces, never into another tenant or cluster-scoped resources.
  • An AppProject per organization limits which repositories it deploys from, which namespaces it deploys to, and which kinds of resources it may create, with RBAC keyed to the organization's group.
  • Still deploys only what was verified. Argo CD syncs digest references, and image admission still rejects anything not signed by release automation (ADR 0005).
  • Owns tenant workloads, not the platform. It owns each tenant service's workload release, refining ADR 0002 and ADR 0011 for tenant workloads only; platform components stay with bootstrap, Helm, and the catalog.

Horizons, in order

From one tenant to a business platform

  1. Now

    Finish Plan 2

    The reference app runs end to end on a fresh profile and passes the portability checks.

  2. Next

    Services are first-class, delivered from git

    Organization and Service exist as platform APIs; Argo CD delivers a Kotlin BFF and a static frontend that reach HTTPS and scale 1→N; Backstage shows them.

  3. Then

    Many tenants, each with its own data

    Two tenants with nested sub-orgs cannot see or reach each other's services or data; object store, Kafka, document, and key-value claims work beside PostgreSQL.

  4. Later

    The one stop shop

    An organization admin and a developer do their whole job in Backstage, through single sign-on, from template to running service.

  5. Someday

    Stronger isolation, a business layer

    A tenant can choose a dedicated virtual or physical cluster; usage becomes reports per organization.

How we will know

Success measures

  • From a Backstage template to a working HTTPS address takes under 15 minutes, with no hand edits to the cluster.
  • A service gets a bucket, a Kafka topic, a document store, a key-value store, and a PostgreSQL database by claim, and a service in another organization cannot reach any of them.
  • Under load, a BFF scales out and back in by itself, and no request fails on a cold pod.
  • An organization admin sees their own tree and nothing else, and a negative test proves it.
  • A new service shows request rate, errors, latency, logs, and traces in its organization's dashboards before anyone writes instrumentation code, and another organization cannot query them.
  • Every workload in every tenant runs by signed digest; no secret exists in plaintext on disk or in git.
  • Moving a service between local and managed changes only environment configuration.
Out of scope

Not goals

  • Not a CI system. Tenants bring their own pipeline; Kore accepts a signed digest.
  • Not a cloud console. A small set of platform APIs, not every upstream knob.
  • Not hiding Kubernetes from operators. The portal hides it from tenants only.
  • Not reimplementing controllers. Tenancy, scaling, hosting, delivery, and data services are composed from upstream controllers.
  • Not forking Backstage or Argo CD. Kore configures them and adds plugins; it carries no patches to them.
Each becomes an ADR before dependent work starts; decided ones link to it
